## Q1 — Published latency of insect leg proprioceptive reflexes

There is **no published loop-latency measurement for the *Drosophila* femur-tibia resistance reflex (FTiR)**. The number that the Drosophila modelling community actually uses is a **sensory-relay delay of 5–15 ms (nominal 10 ms)** and a **motor delay of 20–40 ms (nominal 30 ms)**, adopted by [Karashchuk et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.99005) and explicitly sourced to (i) *Drosophila* leg-mechanosensory monosynaptic relay timing and (ii) force-probe measurements of motor-neuron spike → muscle force in the fly femur. Their Discussion states the sensory figure as "**6 ms from a femoral mechanosensory neuron spike to the onset of a postsynaptic response in the VNC**". I could not obtain the cited Tuthill & Wilson 2016 *Cell* methods to confirm that 6 ms figure at source; the *Cell* paper I did read reports **~3 ms** trial-averaged latency from a single femur-bristle spike to a monosynaptic EPSP ([Tuthill & Wilson 2016, Cell](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4879191/)). Treat the 6 ms and 3 ms numbers as two different publication contexts (a review's cross-species estimate vs. a direct fly measurement), not as a contradiction resolved in the literature.

For the big-insect comparators, the cleanest decomposition published is in [Hölte & Hustert 2003, JEB](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.00492) (locust, abstract read): mechanosensory hairs, campaniform sensilla and spurs of the **distal** leg propagate afferent signals to the CNS in **no less than 8 ms**, whereas **proximal** trochanteral/femoral campaniform sensilla signal distal contact in **~1 ms**, plus **< 1 ms** for cuticular strain transfer. The entire mechano-neuro-muscular pathway from leg tip to neuromuscular synapse is **5–7 ms**, producing tension in the trochanteral depressors essentially at substrate contact. So in the locust, afferent conduction dominates the peripheral delay, and it is **position-dependent** (distal sensors are 8× slower than proximal ones) — a point that matters if the model's proprioceptor is meant to be an FeCO on the femur rather than a tarsal contact sensor. Centrally, a spike in a locust spiking local interneuron produces an IPSP in a nonspiking interneuron **0.6 ± 0.12 ms** later ([Burrows 1987, J Neurosci](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC6569182)), i.e. central synaptic delay is sub-millisecond and is *not* the bottleneck.

For the cockroach, the only whole-animal latency I could verify is behavioural: lateral velocity begins to decrease **13 ± 5 ms** after an impulsive perturbation during running, with recovery in **27 ± 12 ms**, a time described as "comparable with the fastest reflexes measured in cockroaches" ([Jindrich & Full 2002, JEB](http://faculty.washington.edu/minster/bio_inspired_robotics/research_articles/jindrich_full_cockroach_canon_jeb2002.pdf)). Relevant comparison: the cockroach trochanteral hair-plate reflex onto the slow femur-extensor motoneuron is described only qualitatively as "short latency" in the abstract of [Wong & Pearson 1976](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1242/jeb.64.1.233%22&format=json&resultType=core); no number is given there.

For the stick insect FTiR specifically, **the loop latency in milliseconds is not stated** in either of the two sources I could reach. The authoritative review ([Bässler 1993, Brain Res Rev](https://doi.org/10.1016/0165-0173(93)90002-H), abstract only — paywalled) characterises the loop as mainly velocity-sensitive with very high gain, "only a very small phase reserve", working close to instability, but publishes no latency. [Schmitz, Gruhn & Büschges 2019, J Neurophysiol](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC6879953) (abstract only; I was unable to retrieve the full text) reports that active-reaction probability "increases with increasing starting angle and **decreasing stimulus velocity**" — i.e. the loop is a velocity filter, which is a *time-scale* statement, but again no latency in ms. What *is* published for the stick insect are the two peripheral legs of the loop: afferent-to-force latency of **8.5 ms** and a twitch whose half-relaxation is **61 ms** (see table). Any stick-insect FTiR loop latency of ~20–30 ms would have to be assembled by the reader, not quoted.

**Verdict for Q1: the literature supplies a *sensory relay* (~3–6 ms in *Drosophila*, 5–15 ms as used in fly models) and a *motor relay* weirder figure (~30 ms in the fly femur), but no directly measured complete FTiR loop latency for *Drosophila*, and no numeric FTiR loop latency for stick insect in the sources accessible to me.**

## Q2 — Time constants of proprioceptor adaptation / encoding in insect legs

The honest answer: **explicit adaptation time constants (τ in ms) for insect leg chordotonal organs or hair plates are essentially not published.** [Mamiya et al. 2018, Neuron](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6481666/) — the primary *Drosophila* FeCO study — classifies FeCO axons into "**two tonic (non-adapting) and three phasic (adapting)**" subclasses using GCaMP6f calcium imaging during servo-controlled joint movements at 360–800°/s and ramp-and-hold steps of 18° every step, but reports **no adaptation time constant, no response latency, and no calcium-decay τ**; the response delay is buried in the GCaMP6f indicator kinetics, which the authors do not model. The companion biomechanics paper [Mamiya et al. 2023, Neuron](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10644877/) likewise reports neural coding and biomechanics but no τ.

What *is* quantified about encoding speed is kinematic, not temporal-constant-like: claw neurons respond tonically and their steady-state activity is a roughly linear function of joint angle with hysteresis (bigger steady-state response when the joint reached a position in the activating direction); club neurons' calcium slope peaks around **400°/s** and declines slightly at higher speeds; hook neurons' calcium slope is **flat across 100–800°/s** ([Mamiya et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6481666/)). Club neurons respond tonically to vibration and are frequency-tuned with peaks at **400 Hz** (0.9 µm amplitude) and **800 Hz** (0.054 µm). Single club neurons are tuned to individual frequencies between **200 and 1600 Hz**.

Analogous numbers from other insects that *are* numbers, but for non-leg proprioceptors: cockroach trochanteral hair-plate sensilla split into **type I (dynamic-only)** and **type II (dynamic + static)** responders ([Wong & Pearson 1976](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1242/jeb.64.1.233%22&format=json&resultType=core), abstract). Hawkmoth Johnston's organ neurons encode both angular velocity and position with quantified adaptation and non-linear hysteresis during ramp-and-hold stimuli ([Mukunda & Sane 2025, JEB 228:jeb249342](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12079665)) — but I could not extract the numeric τ from the accessible text.

**Verdict for Q2: no published adaptation time constant for any insect leg proprioceptor could be located. The closest defensible statement is qualitative and categorical: *Drosophila* FeCO encodes joint position with non-adapting (tonic) responses in claw neurons, and movement phasically in hook/club neurons. A 5–20 ms exponential filter cannot be justified or rejected from this literature.**

## Q3 — Twitch rise and relaxation time constants of insect leg muscles

For *Drosophila* leg muscles, the **twitch time course in ms is not published.** [Dorsett 1990](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1007/BF01843574%22&format=json&resultType=core) measured the mechanics of the dorsal longitudinal flight muscle (asynchronous) and the tergal depressor of the trochanter (TDT, a leg muscle, synchronous) in wild-type flies, and the abstract reports only *qualitative* and *relative* facts: both flight muscles had high relaxed stiffness, low steady-state active tension, and a large well-maintained delayed tension increase after stretch, with a rate constant ~**10× greater** in *D. melanogaster* than in *Lethocerus*; the leg muscles had lower relaxed stiffness, full Ca²⁺ activation, and a small, poorly maintained delayed tension increase, with a rate constant similar between species. **No ms values, no twitch rise time, no relaxation τ for either muscle.** For the fly femur-tibia muscle, the only millisecond figure I could verify is the motor-neuron-spike-to-peak-force delay of **~30 ms**, with the tested motor-delay range 20–40 ms ([Azevedo et al. 2020, eLife](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7347388/), as stated by [Karashchuk et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.99005)).

For insect leg muscles generally, the numbers are good and they are **slow**:

- Stick insect *Carausius morosus* extensor tibiae single twitch: latency **8.5 ms**, T_max **26.4 ms**, T_50%off **60.7 ms**, T_90%off **149.9 ms**, full return to baseline **~250 ms**; flexor tibiae is ~3× faster to peak (**8–10 ms**) and 3–6× faster relaxing (**24–51 ms**) (Guschlbauer thesis, Tables C.3/C.4 and Results).
- Stick insect extensor tibiae, isotonic, driven by physiologically patterned input: relaxation **τ ≈ 55 ms**, plateau before rapid relaxation **64 ± 14 ms** ([Hooper et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.01283.2006), abstract; reproduced in Guschlbauer thesis). Isometric relaxation τ spans **30.9 ms (50 Hz) to 145.7 ms (200 Hz)**, with delay-to-relaxation 33.4–109.7 ms across 30–200 Hz (Guschlbauer thesis, Discussion). Contraction time constants are **200–700 ms** and are described as "always extremely slow" ([Hooper et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.01283.2006), abstract).
- Locust hind-leg extensor tibiae: time to peak **67 ± 9 ms** (slow MN) and **61 ± 6 ms** (fast MN); a single spike changes force for **≥ 200 ms**, decay up to **~1 s** ([Harischandra et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6812852/)). Their fitted Wilson non-linear activation model's τ_c is on the order of **70–150 ms** across animals (Table 2) — i.e. "activation dynamics" in insects is a **tens-of-milliseconds** process, not a millisecond-scale one.
- Slow (postural) vs fast: the cleanest published contrast is *within* the stick insect extensor tibiae (slow vs fast fibre types and slow vs fast motoneuron) and the *between-species* comparison in the Guschlbauer thesis (Table D.10, abstract read): T_max is very similar for *C. morosus* and *Blaberus discoidalis*, *Periplaneta americana* develops max twitch force in **half** as much time, and the largest differences are in the **relaxation** phase (T_50%off ≈ ⅔ in *B. discoidalis*, < ½ in *P. americana*). Asynchronous flight muscle twitches are longer still than the stick insect leg extensor, which "relaxes twice as fast as" the beetle *Cotinus* basalar flight muscle (Guschlbauer thesis, Discussion).

**Verdict for Q3: for *Drosophila* leg muscles specifically, the ms-scale twitch time course has not been published; the only *Drosophila* leg-muscle timing figure I can cite is ~30 ms from motor-neuron spike to peak force. For insect leg muscles in general, published values cluster in the tens of milliseconds (25–150 ms to peak/relax in stick insect and locust leg muscles, with slower contraction τ of 200–700 ms under patterned activation).**

## Q4 — Any *Drosophila* measurement of proprioceptive-stimulus → motor-neuron → movement latency?

There is **partial** evidence, all of it sub-loop:

1. **Calcium imaging, proprioceptor → VNC, no motor neuron endpoint.** [Mamiya et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6481666/) and [Mamiya et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10644877/) image FeCO axons and claw cell bodies during servo-controlled passive femur-tibia movements (100–800°/s swings, 18° ramp-and-hold steps, vibration), and [Agrawal et al. 2020, eLife](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7752136/) and [Dallmann et al. 2024, Nature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10634730.2/) image downstream VNC neurons. **None of these papers report a latency in ms** from stimulus onset to response. The response timing is explicitly limited by GCaMP6f indicator kinetics, which the authors do not convert to a delay.
2. **Electrophysiology, mechanoreceptor → VNC neuron: ~3 ms.** Trial-averaged latency of a monosynaptic EPSP in three classes of VNC neuron after a single bristle-afferent spike, SD typically <1 ms ([Tuthill & Wilson 2016, Cell](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4879191/)). This is the most directly usable *Drosophila* latency number. It is a **touch** (bristle), not a chordotonal, afferent.
3. **Motor neuron → force: ~30 ms.** Spike-to-peak-force in a fly femur muscle via a calibrated force probe ([Azevedo et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7347388/), per [Karashchuk et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.99005)). Note Azevedo 2020 also reports the tibia *starts to move* ~**10 ms** after stimulation onset in a stick insect high-speed-tracking analogue only (Guschlbauer thesis); I did not find the equivalent explicit fly number.
4. **Anatomical (not temporal) evidence for a short loop.** Connectomic reconstruction shows claw and hook FeCO axons synapse **directly onto leg motor neurons** and via dedicated premotor interneurons with a modular "one FeCO subtype → one motor module" organisation ([Lee et al. 2025, Nat Commun 16:4105](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12048489/)). This bounds the loop at **2 synapses minimum**, which with a ~0.6 ms central synaptic delay ([Burrows 1987](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC6569182)) and ~6 ms afferent relay ([Tuthill & Wilson 2016](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC5120761)) implies a reflex arc substantially shorter than 20 ms, but **no one has measured it end-to-end**.

**Verdict for Q4: there is no published end-to-end proprioceptive-stimulus-to-movement latency for *Drosophila* legs. The measurable components are ~3 ms (mechanoreceptor spike → VNC EPSP), ~6 ms (cited femoral mechanosensory → VNC response onset), and ~30 ms (motor neuron spike → peak muscle force).**

## Q5 — Is there a published rule of the form "a motor command filter should not be faster than the reflex loop delay"?

**No.** No paper I read proposes that rule. The closest things that exist are:

- **A constraint-by-delay-budget result, not a filtering rule.** [Karashchuk et al. 2024, eLife](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.99005) built a 3-D kinematic *Drosophila* walking model with explicit sensory and motor delays and found that kinematically realistic walking under stochastic perturbations survives **up to ~30 ms of motor delay, ~10 ms of sensory delay, or ~45 ms combined**; they also found that performance depends on the **sum** of the two delays, and that "for low values of motor delay (10 ms, 20 ms), even stronger perturbations had almost no effect". They tested sensory delays of 0–15 ms and motor delays of 10–40 ms, so **they never tested a condition in which a small delay (≤5 ms) was harmful.** The paper contains no statement that a filter must be slower than the loop, and their own best-performing regimes include the fastest delays tested.
- **A delay-budget decomposition, not a rule.** [Tuthill & Wilson 2016, Curr Biol](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC5120761) state that an insect reacts to a mechanical stimulus within **20–30 ms**, of which **6–8 ms** is mechanosensory transduction plus axonal conduction and **10 ms** is muscle force-production kinetics. This is the quantitative backbone one would use to *argue* for a floor on any motor-command low-pass filter — a filter much faster than the muscle it drives passes dynamics the muscle cannot realise — but the review does not state that argument.
- **Mechanistic, not normative, evidence that the actuator is the slow element.** Contraction time constants of insect leg muscle under patterned activation are **200–700 ms** and relaxation τ is **30–150 ms** ([Hooper et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.01283.2006); Guschlbauer thesis). A 20 ms command filter is therefore still ~2–7× faster than the muscle's own relaxation, and 10× faster than its contraction τ. This is a defensible *physical* argument for 20 ms over 5 ms, but it is my synthesis of published numbers, not a published rule.
- **A structural hint that the sensory side is pre-filtered by biomechanics.** *Drosophila* claw proprioceptors' joint-angle selectivity is set by tendon/arculum mechanics, not ion-channel kinetics ([Mamiya et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10644877/)), and the medial tendon reduces on-axis movement to claw dendrites by as much as **30×**, with claw mechanosensory threshold >10× higher than club neurons. So a real fly already has a mechanical low-pass in series with the proprioceptor.

**Verdict for Q5: the rule you are looking for does not exist in the literature. There is corroborating quantitative material (delay budgets, muscle time constants) but no citation that will say "your filter must be ≥ X ms".**

## WHAT THIS DOES NOT SUPPORT

**The literature does not justify a specific numeric value for a 5 ms versus a 20 ms motor-command filter.** Stated plainly:

1. There is **no published *Drosophila* femur-tibia resistance-reflex loop latency**. The nearest published quantities are a **~3 ms** monosynaptic mechanoreceptor→VNC EPSP latency ([Tuthill & Wilson 2016, Cell](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4879191/)), a **6 ms** femoral-mechanosensory→VNC-response-onset figure cited by [Karashchuk et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.99005), a **5–8 ms** locust distal-afferent propagation time ([Hölte & Hustert 2003](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.00492)), and a **~30 ms** spike-to-peak-force delay in the fly leg ([Azevedo et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7347388/)). Assembling these into a loop gives roughly **35–45 ms**, which is *longer* than either candidate filter — so a loop-latency argument would license a filter **slower** than 20 ms, not a choice between 5 and 20.
2. There is **no published adaptation time constant for any insect leg proprioceptor** (Q2). Nothing in the sensory-encoding literature constrains the filter.
3. There is **no published *Drosophila* leg-muscle twitch time constant in ms** (Q3). The generic insect-leg numbers (**25–150 ms** to peak/relax; **200–700 ms** contraction τ) point at muscle being the dominant slow element, which is an argument that **20 ms is more plausible than 5 ms** — but that is a *synthesis across species*, principally stick insect and locust, and it is not a *Drosophila* measurement.
4. The single most authoritative, directly-relevant modelling paper ([Karashchuk et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.99005)) **tested delay values and found the fast end of its range benign**, and never examined a command *filter time constant* at all. It cannot be cited for "the filter should be 20 ms".

**What you can defensibly write, with citations:**

> The spike-to-muscle pathway in a fly leg has published delays of ~6 ms from femoral mechanosensory neuron to VNC postsynaptic response, and ~30 ms from motor-neuron spike to peak force in a femur muscle [Karashchuk et al. 2024, citing Tuthill & Wilson 2016 and Azevedo et al. 2020]; insect leg muscles themselves contract and relax with time constants of tens to hundreds of milliseconds (relaxation τ ≈ 55 ms isotonic, 31–146 ms isometric, contraction τ 200–700 ms in stick insect extensor tibiae; time-to-peak 61–67 ms in locust extensor tibiae) [Hooper et al. 2007; Harischandra et al. 2019]. We therefore set our population-rate filter time constant to 20 ms, which is faster than the measured muscle relaxation time constant and slower than the measured sensory relay, and we note that **no published measurement determines this value for *Drosophila***.

That is the strongest claim the literature supports. It supports **20 ms over 5 ms** on actuator-physics grounds, weakly and by cross-species analogy; it supports **neither** value as a measured *Drosophila* number.

## DISAGREEMENTS AND GAPS

**Genuine disagreements / ambiguities in the literature**

1. **The *Drosophila* sensory-relay number is quoted two ways.** [Karashchuk et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.99005) uses 5–15 ms (nominal 10 ms) and cites Tuthill & Wilson 2016 for a 6 ms value; the *Cell* paper by the same first author reports **~3 ms** for a monosynaptic bristle→VNC EPSP. These are different measurements (review-level cross-species estimate vs. a single identified touch afferent), but a reader who takes the fly model's numbers as directly measured would be mis-citing. I could not access the full text of the cited source to resolve it.
2. **Afferent delay is strongly position-dependent in insects** — ≥8 ms from distal leg mechanoreceptors vs ~1 ms from proximal trochanteral campaniform sensilla ([Hölte & Hustert 2003](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.00492)). Any single "sensory delay" for a leg is therefore a choice about *which sensor*, not a species constant.
3. **Muscle time constants depend massively on protocol.** Isotonic relaxation τ ≈ 55 ms vs. isometric 31–146 ms; delay-to-relaxation 33–110 ms depending on stimulation frequency and spike number. Hooper et al. explicitly report that contraction and relaxation τ differ and are history-dependent, which means "the muscle time constant" is not a single number even in one muscle.
4. **Fast vs slow muscle types** are clearly distinguished functionally (fast MN vs slow MN; flight vs leg) but the quantitative contrast for *Drosophila* is not published — the Dorsett 1990 abstract gives only relative rate constants and cross-species ratios.

**Explicit gaps (not published, as far as I could find)**

- No measured *Drosophila* FTiR loop latency (stimulus → motor neuron → force → movement).
- No measured adaptation time constant (τ) for any insect leg chordotonal organ or hair plate, and specifically none for *Drosophila* FeCO claw/hook/club neurons. All FeCO response timing is confounded by GCaMP kinetics.
- No measured *Drosophila* leg-muscle twitch rise/decay time in ms. Only the ~30 ms spike-to-peak-force delay for one femur muscle.
- No published sensory transduction *delay* for chordotonal neurons in ms separated from conduction (the 6–8 ms figure in [Tuthill & Wilson 2016](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC5120761) is given as a combined "transduction and axonal conduction" budget, not decomposed).
- No cochroach or stick-insect FTiR *loop* latency in ms anywhere I could reach; [Bässler 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/0165-0173(93)90002-H) and [Schmitz et al. 2019](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC6879953) were abstract-only for me, and neither abstract contains a latency number.
- No paper proposes a criterion relating a motor-command filter time constant to a reflex loop delay.

**Access limitations I hit (so you can weigh the above):** Bässler 1993 (Brain Res Rev) — abstract only; Schmitz et al. 2019 (J Neurophysiol) — abstract only, full text blocked; Wong & Pearson 1976 (JEB) — abstract only; Dorsett 1990 (J Muscle Res Cell Motil) — abstract only; Tuthill & Wilson 2016 *Curr Biol* review — full text not retrievable (PMC HTML and publisher both blocked), so the 6–8 ms / 10 ms / 20–30 ms decomposition is quoted via its abstract and via Karashchuk et al. 2024; Hölte & Hustert 2003 (JEB) — abstract only; Malamud & Josephson and Josephson muscle papers — not retrievable, so slow-vs-fast flight-muscle comparisons here rest on the Guschlbauer thesis's citations rather than on the primary papers.
